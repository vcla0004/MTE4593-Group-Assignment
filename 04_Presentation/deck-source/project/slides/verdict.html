Victor (about 1 minute), then close. Triple bottom line. Environmental is mixed. Per kWh it is excellent: up to about 1.5 million tonnes of CO₂e a year if one unit replaced today’s NSW grid mix, though less by the time it could actually run, around 2040. LC3 trims construction emissions further. But per dollar it loses: in every Liddell case we tested, spending the same money on firmed renewables avoids more CO₂e by 2050 than one SMR, because renewables can be built sooner. Economic is weak: SMRs are the highest-cost option in GenCost. At GenCost costs, the same spend buys about 5 times the energy as firmed renewables (1.3 times in the worst case); on Darlington’s own capital cost (C$20.9 billion, about A$23 billion) it is about 2 times, and NuScale shows the first-of-a-kind cost risk. (Provisional: inputs not yet confirmed in primary sources; see 03_LCA_Calculations/results/same_money.md.) Social is mixed: 61% national support, but a legal ban, waste concerns and no host-community consent yet. Our conclusion: SMRs are very low-carbon per kWh, but for Australia they are too costly and arrive too late to be the best climate investment, and the law, financing and community consent would all have to change first. Our materials idea, LC3 concrete for Darlington Units 2 to 4, is worth adopting only if it is schedule-neutral, for example by qualifying the mix on mock-ups while Unit 1 is being built. Even a short delay means more gas generation in the meantime, which could outweigh the concrete saving. The biggest climate lever is where the reactor goes and how fast it runs. “Thank you. We’re happy to take questions.” (These ratings are our preliminary judgement; the report’s weighted multi-criteria comparison will replace them.) LIKELY QUESTIONS (all three of you should know these): 1. Why compare with average grid power, not the plant being replaced? Because the average factor is the published, comparable figure. Replacing coal specifically would save even more. 2. Is LC3 allowed in nuclear concrete? Not yet proven: it must be qualified under Canada’s CSA N287 standards. That is our stated research need, and why we target Units 2–4, which leaves time to test it. 3. Why not just build renewables? GenCost says firmed renewables are cheaper today. SMRs offer firm power and a small land footprint, but at much higher cost. 4. What about waste? It has to be planned up front; the PNAS study suggests more waste by volume per unit of energy, which NuScale disputes. 5. How long until Australia could have one? Law change, then licensing (2.5 years in Canada), then construction (licence in April 2025 to expected service in October 2030 at Darlington, so about 5 years): so 2040 at the earliest (CSIRO), probably later.
