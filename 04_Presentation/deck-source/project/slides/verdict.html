Victor (about 1 minute), then close. Triple bottom line. Environmental is strong: about 1.5 million tonnes of CO₂e a year avoided if one unit replaces NSW coal, and coal ash reused instead of left in dams. Economic is weak: SMRs are the highest-cost option in GenCost, and NuScale shows first-of-a-kind cost risk. Social is mixed: 61% national support, but a legal ban, waste concerns and no host-community consent yet. Our conclusion: SMRs are a strong climate option for Australia only if the law, financing and community consent change, and none of that is quick. Our materials idea, reclaimed coal ash in the plant’s concrete, is useful regardless, because it applies to any large concrete build on a former coal site. “Thank you. We’re happy to take questions.” (These ratings are our preliminary judgement; the report’s weighted multi-criteria comparison will replace them.) LIKELY QUESTIONS (all three of you should know these): 1. Why compare with average grid power, not the plant being replaced? Because the average factor is the published, comparable figure. Replacing coal specifically would save even more. 2. Is fly ash allowed in nuclear concrete? Supplementary cementitious materials are used in large concrete pours generally, but nuclear-grade use must be qualified. That is our stated R&D need. 3. Why not just build renewables? GenCost says firmed renewables are cheaper today. SMRs offer firm power and a small land footprint, but at much higher cost. 4. What about waste? It has to be planned up front; the PNAS study suggests more waste by volume per unit of energy, which NuScale disputes. 5. How long until Australia could have one? Law change, then licensing (2.5 years in Canada), then construction (licence in April 2025 to expected operation at the end of 2029 at Darlington, so about 4.5 years): realistically the late 2030s at the earliest.
