## Slide 9: Location (about 1 minute)
Thanks, [Leroy]. So: we know what the reactor emits. The bigger question is **what it replaces**.

This is our own calculation. One BWRX-300 makes about 2.4 terawatt-hours a year. In New South Wales, every kilowatt-hour on the grid emits about 640 grams of CO₂. Our reactor emits about 4. So one unit would avoid about **one and a half million tonnes** of CO₂ a year.

In Ontario, the grid is already mostly hydro and nuclear. The same reactor saves only 70 to 160 thousand tonnes. [Pause] That's **at least nine times less**, for exactly the same machine.

But here's the catch. Australia couldn't have one running before about **2040**, and by then our grid will be much cleaner. So location matters, but **speed** matters just as much.

And what slows it down here? There are four barriers.

## Slide 10: Law (about 1 minute)
First: it's **illegal**.

Three laws stand in the way at Liddell. Section 140A of the EPBC Act stops the Environment Minister approving a nuclear power plant. Section 10 of the ARPANS Act stops the national regulator licensing one. And New South Wales has its own ban on nuclear power reactors.

Repeal isn't enough on its own. Our regulator can only license Commonwealth bodies, so a private operator would need a new licensing system. Politically, repeal needs a Senate majority, and right now neither Labor nor the Greens support it.

For scale: in Canada, with an experienced regulator, the construction licence alone took **29 months**.

So the fix here isn't engineering. It's legislation and a capable regulator. And even if the law changed, there's the cost.

## Slide 11: Cost (about 1 minute)
CSIRO's GenCost ranks small modular reactors as the **most expensive** new power in Australia. That's even after including Darlington's real costs. Firmed renewables are the cheapest.

The warning from the US is NuScale. Their planned price rose from 58 to **89 dollars** a megawatt-hour, and that was *after* about 30 dollars of subsidies. Not enough buyers signed up, and the project was cancelled in 2023.

The lesson: the first plants carry huge cost risk, and someone has to wear it. In Canada, the government is taking equity in Darlington to do exactly that.

Cost is one thing. But does the public want it?

## Slide 12: Social licence (about 1 minute)
Actually, most Australians **do**: 61 per cent supported nuclear power in the 2024 Lowy poll.

And on safety, the numbers are striking. [Pause] Per unit of electricity, nuclear has caused about **0.03 deaths**, including Chernobyl and Fukushima. Coal causes about **25**, mostly from air pollution. The risk people fear isn't the one that actually kills.

But national support isn't the same as a community saying yes. A plant at Liddell would need real consent from the Hunter community and First Nations, a waste plan agreed up front, and local benefit sharing. Canada shows it can work: First Nations there have lent money to Darlington that turns into ownership.

Last barrier: where the fuel comes from.

## Slide 13: Fuel supply (about 1 minute)
Australia has the **most uranium in the world**: about 28 per cent. But we just dig it up and ship it. We don't convert or enrich any of it.

Enrichment is dominated by very few players, and Russia alone has about **43 per cent**. We measured how concentrated the market is with the Herfindahl-Hirschman index. Anything above 1,800 counts as highly concentrated, and enrichment comes out at about **3,000**.

So an Australian reactor would depend on a handful of overseas suppliers. Long-term contracts with allied enrichers reduce that risk, but they don't remove it.

So, putting it all together: is it worth it?

## Slide 14: Verdict (about 1 minute)
Across the triple bottom line:

**Environmentally**, it's mixed. Per kilowatt-hour, nuclear is excellent. But per **dollar**, it loses: in every case we tested, spending the same money on firmed renewables avoided more CO₂ by 2050, because renewables can be built sooner.

**Economically**, it's weak. It's the most expensive option.

**Socially**, it's mixed. There's support, but there's also a legal ban, waste concerns, and no community consent yet.

So our conclusion: for Australia, SMRs are very low-carbon, but **too costly and too late** to be the best climate investment.

And our materials idea, LC3 concrete at Darlington? It's worth doing, but **only if it doesn't delay construction**. Our numbers show that just 9 to 15 days of delay per reactor would wipe out the whole saving. [Pause] The biggest climate lever isn't the concrete. It's **where** the reactor goes, and **how fast** it runs.

Thank you. We're happy to take questions.
