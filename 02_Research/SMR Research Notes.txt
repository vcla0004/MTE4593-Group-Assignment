1/09 thoughts

Thoughts

SMRs are the subject of research and implementation in technologically innovative countries like China, the US. Geopolitically, there is perhaps an element of ‘arms race’ to this, though this might just be because of reminiscence. Does the sovereign nature of scientific research overcome this (opensource, atomic spies)? In China it’s likely politically driven (a la huge infrastructure projects like belt and road, but also precise sectioning of cities specialised for certain manufacturing, and also sustainability focus), whereas in the US it’s probably government contracts to corporations (does the huge oil industry, and petrodollars underpinning their economy discourage alternate energy innovation?).

Australia specific.

Australia suffers from a soft resources trap (if DRC has a hard resources trap), where natural gas is sold off to Japan et cetera cheaply (US influence?)iron ore is merely extracted and shipped off to China, not even being reduced or manufactured. Talks of course of green steel, hydrogen exportation even.

Hmm, the notion that ‘energy can’t be transported like materials can’ is true for some, false for others. Oil, gas et cetera can be shipped. Electricity from solar panels suffers from battery problems (though China solved this with swappable batteries for electric cars -> seems very modular/right to repair informed design thinking). SMRs seem innately local. Australia has a lot of Uranium deposits. Will larger structures preserve local, microgridesque SMRs, or stack them together like RAM at a datacentre? For me, social sustainability underpins environmental sustainability, so an open-sourced SMR seems more likely to deliver on that.

I was considering voting for Peter Dutton last election because a significant part of his campaign was SMRs. Albanese won but he invested in the AUKUS submarine development for the US (they use nuclear engines, aka much of the same technology as SMRs). I don’t think it’s a coincidence, I think this is orchestrated, whether by the US for colony militarisation, or Australia to gain geopolitical leverage through energy sovereignty. Anyway, this opens a wider conversation about war, and how much of innovation stems from it.

Australia has a nuclear fear problem from propaganda around the 70s, Fukushima, et cetera. How accurately can an average consumer predict the safety and sustainability of SMRs and coal, vs. the actual values?

Important to note that SMRs currently differ from conventional energy sources in that while they are small and theoretically producible by university-scale research organisations, the technology itself (not the fuel source) is the uncertain/scarce point, and thus the most valuable. The more interesting conversation is perhaps not the extraction (uranium follows similar colonial/military stories of lithium, oil, et cetera), but the intellectual property. Will we be paying an IP tax every kW we consume? If we’re using US vs. Chinese technology.
