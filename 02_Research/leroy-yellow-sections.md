### 3.4.1 Stakeholder identification and responsibilities

The sustainability of the proposed concrete initiative depends on more than the material’s laboratory CO ₂ performance. At Darlington, the decision to use limestone–calcined-clay cement (LC³), a qualified slag-blended binder or conventional concrete would affect construction risk, procurement, community confidence and long-term liability. Stakeholder analysis is therefore undertaken at two distinct scales: (1) the actual Darlington New Nuclear Project (DNNP) and its proposed materials pilot; and (2) the hypothetical Liddell, New South Wales, reactor used for Australian policy comparison. Decisions made by Ontario Power Generation (OPG) cannot be assumed to apply to Liddell. The primary decision-maker is OPG, the site owner and licensee, working with its engineering design authority and construction contractors. The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) independently determines whether licensed nuclear safety requirements are satisfied. GE Vernova Hitachi and qualified suppliers influence the reactor design and available materials. Indigenous Nations, local residents, workers, ratepayers, cement producers and waste-management organisations experience different benefits and risks. ### 3.4.2 Indigenous participation, ownership and social legitimacy

The Darlington area is on the traditional lands and waters of the Michi Saagiig Anishinaabeg associated with the Williams Treaties. In June 2026, the Williams Treaties First Nations announced an investment in the Darlington New Nuclear Project supported by approximately C$715 million in federal and provincial loan-guarantee financing. The Canadian government's published project overview identifies an arrangement designed to support a meaningful minority ownership position (Government of Canada 2026; CNSC 2026a). The investment illustrates a more substantive form of participation than consultation alone: Indigenous communities may acquire enduring financial interests in infrastructure affecting their traditional territory. Nevertheless, ownership by participating nations cannot be treated as universal consent to all activities or as a substitute for consultation with other potentially affected Indigenous groups. The sourcing of calcined clay, haulage routes, construction water use and waste handling may create additional, distributional impacts. These matters remain relevant even when the finished concrete has a lower modelled embodied-carbon intensity. A socially sustainable LC³ intervention would therefore involve early disclosure of proposed source locations and transport routes, opportunities for Indigenous and local suppliers where commercially and technically suitable, independently understandable material and environmental reporting, and documented responses to concerns. The proposed material's occupational implications also warrant assessment, including dust exposure during powder handling, calcination-related emissions at supplier facilities, and any changes in on-site batching or curing practices.

### 3.5 Market

Analysis and Economic Feasibility

### 3.5.1 SMR market conditions and entry barriers

SMR commercialisation involves substantial up-front capital, multi-year development periods, demanding component qualification and regulatory uncertainty. A smaller reactor module does not necessarily mean proportionally cheaper electricity: site works, site security, licensing and other fixed costs remain significant, and promised serial-manufacturing savings depend on sufficient repeat orders. For early deployments, cost and completion estimates carry greater uncertainty than for mature construction programmes (IAEA 2023; CSIRO 2026). Market capitalisation is an unsuitable proxy for either reactor construction cost or demonstrated operating performance. The share price of a technology company reflects investor expectations, financing conditions and the market's assessment of future opportunities, rather than verified capital intensity, reactor availability or electricity production costs. An economic comparison is more appropriately based on total project cost, construction schedule, cost overruns, annual net generation, maintenance, fuel costs, financing terms and levelised or system-level electricity costs. The BWRX-300 at Darlington is a stronger benchmark than a hypothetical vendor valuation because the owner and government have disclosed a programme-level investment estimate, although actual operating performance has yet to be established.

### 3.5.2 Project financing, investment and money flows

Canada's Major Projects Office reports a C$20.9 billion programme budget for four planned BWRX-300 units at Darlington and approximately C$7.7 billion for the first unit including common infrastructure. The first-unit amount is contained within the wider programme budget and cannot be added to it a second time. Announced commitments include up to C$2 billion through the Canada Growth Fund and up to C$1 billion through the Building Ontario Fund. Loan guarantees of approximately C$715 million support Indigenous investment financing; these guarantees are credit support, not another C$715 million of construction spending (Government of Canada 2026). The flow of economic value is therefore more complex than a single company financing a plant and selling electricity. Public institutions and investors provide equity and credit support; OPG and contracted delivery organisations procure engineering services and qualified materials; component and concrete manufacturers receive contractual revenue; electricity consumers face the longer-term price and system consequences; and participating shareholders bear risks and may receive financial returns. This structure can reduce financing obstacles but transfers exposure to public or quasi-public balance sheets. Financial sustainability must therefore assess the opportunity cost of capital alongside potential decarbonisation and industrial benefits.

### 3.5.3 Economic sensitivity of the low-carbon concrete intervention

The LC³ decision is economically narrower than the decision to build a nuclear reactor. The relevant comparison is the incremental cost of a qualified and delivered concrete mix, not the total C$20.9 billion programme budget. The cost difference may include binder price, transport, admixtures, testing, independent assurance, additional batching controls, schedule effects and any avoided rework or maintenance. For the central illustrative volume of 20,000 m³, a hypothetical premium of C$10/m³ generates C$200,000 in material-only additional cost. Dividing this by the 2,200-tonne conditional embodied saving produces a material-only abatement cost of about C$91/t CO ₂ -eq. At C$25/m³, the corresponding figures are C$500,000 and C$227/t. No supplier quotation has been obtained for these premiums, so they are sensitivity values rather than assertions about the prevailing Ontario LC³ price. A full procurement decision must also include testing and timing effects.

### 3.5.4 Supply-chain concentration, fuel sovereignty and industrial capability

Nuclear energy independence cannot be inferred solely from ownership of uranium deposits. Commercial nuclear generation depends on uranium conversion, enrichment, fuel assembly fabrication, specialised forged components, intellectual property, spare parts and skilled labour. These stages have different geographic concentrations and can face trade or export restrictions. Public patent information can identify proprietary technologies, but it does not establish any particular licensing royalty or recurring payment per unit of electricity. The World Nuclear Association's published 2025 enrichment-capacity inventory reports 68,076 thousand separative work units annually, with major contributions from Rosatom, Urenco, CNNC and Orano. Using the stated operator capacities and the standard Herfindahl–Hirschman formula, HHI = Σ(100 si)², where si is each supplier's share of total capacity, gives approximately 3,009. This indicates a concentrated market at the level of the reported operators. The calculation describes nominal production capacity rather than contracts available to any individual buyer; country restrictions, enrichment services, fuel fabrication and actual trade flows can create further constraints (World Nuclear Association 2026). For Darlington, an established Canadian nuclear ecosystem and qualified vendors may reduce some supply and implementation barriers. For a hypothetical Australian project, domestic construction capabilities and fuel procurement would require development or long-term cross-border agreements. For the LC³ material change itself, supply robustness depends on the location and availability of suitable calcined clay, blending capacity, quality-controlled batching, transport emissions and supplier redundancy. A single qualified concrete supplier could introduce risks even if its advertised product footprint were favourable.

### 3.6 Government

Regulations Review

### 3.6.1 Canadian nuclear approval framework

The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission regulates nuclear facilities under the Nuclear Safety and Control Act and associated regulations. Licensing, inspection, regulatory hold points and public hearings provide independent oversight of design and construction. At Darlington, OPG holds a construction licence for one BWRX-300 granted in April 2025, and the first construction hold point was removed in March 2026. OPG’s application to operate the first unit, submitted in March 2026, is a separate regulatory process. Possible future Units 2–4 are not automatically covered by the first-unit construction licence (CNSC 2026a, 2026b). These distinctions affect the timing and feasibility of the C1 materials proposal. A material selected for an ordinary, non-safety-rated auxiliary structure will be reviewed against different requirements from material incorporated into a safety-significant reactor-building component. Both applications need sound engineering documentation, but the latter may affect the licensed design basis and require a more extensive demonstration. A lower carbon footprint cannot override acceptance criteria for structural strength, corrosion resistance, crack control or design-life performance.

### 3.6.2 Concrete qualification and configuration control

CNSC REGDOC-2.5.2, version 2.1, establishes risk-informed requirements for the design of water-cooled reactor facilities, including classification and engineering documentation for structures, systems and components. The CSA N287 series addresses concrete containment structures, where applicable. The exact edition and controlling clauses must be determined from the project licensing basis, structure classification and approved design documents; it would be incorrect to apply every nuclear containment standard to every site pavement or non-safety-related foundation (CNSC 2023; CSA Group 2026). For a candidate LC³ mix, engineering acceptance depends on evidence of constituent traceability, production consistency, compressive strength development, workability and filling ability, heat of hydration in mass pours, shrinkage, cracking, permeability, chloride exposure, freeze–thaw resistance and compatibility with steel reinforcement or plates. Where a structure performs a nuclear safety function, additional long-term verification and change-control documentation are necessary. The material would first be screened for eligible non-safety uses, then considered for more demanding applications only after suitable mock-ups, laboratory results and authorised engineering review. Regulatory oversight is a continuing requirement, not a one-off certificate. Batch testing, record retention, nonconformance procedures, contractor qualification and inspection access are part of the demonstrable quality system. A conventional approved mix should remain available where an alternative has not satisfied all acceptance criteria. The proposed sequence supports decarbonisation while retaining the regulator's priority of protecting workers, communities and the environment.

### 3.6.3 Environmental law and embodied-carbon procurement policy

Material approval has both technical and environmental dimensions. The relevant environmental comparison includes clay extraction, calcination fuels, grinding, clinker production, admixture use, delivery distances, rejected batches and end-of-life handling. A lower factory-gate number cannot be treated as a project-wide benefit if upstream or transport burdens are excluded inconsistently. Supplier EPDs should follow comparable product-category and lifecycle disclosure rules. Canada's federal Standard on Embodied Carbon in Construction, revised in March 2025, incorporates requirements for relevant federal construction procurement, including low-carbon concrete, lifecycle assessment and structural material reporting (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat 2025). This is evidence of a wider procurement trend and a useful model for transparency; it is not automatically a mandatory specification for OPG merely because OPG is an Ontario provincial utility. The Darlington material choice remains dependent on its own construction contracts, codes, licence conditions and environmental commitments. Public and Indigenous participation also forms part of legitimate project governance. Community interests in local materials extraction, truck traffic, air quality, water, waste handling and nuclear emergency preparedness should be addressed through accessible assessment material and opportunities for response. Consultation does not transfer licensing responsibilities from the CNSC to affected parties, but can identify impacts that technical or cost-only analysis would otherwise miss.

### 3.6.4 Decommissioning and waste stewardship

End-of-life obligations are relevant to the sustainability of every nuclear reactor, regardless of whether its concrete binder contains less clinker. Decommissioning requires planned shutdown, defuelling, radiation surveys, dismantling, classification of contaminated or activated materials, waste processing and site restoration under regulatory oversight. Clean steel and concrete may be suitable for reuse or conventional recycling after regulatory clearance; radioactive components must be managed through appropriate pathways. The reactor operator must also provide for long-term liabilities in its financial arrangements. Canada's Nuclear Waste Management Organization is progressing a deep geological repository for used nuclear fuel in the Wabigoon Lake Ojibway Nation–Ignace area. The project entered a regulatory assessment process in January 2026 and is not an operating repository. The long-term management of spent fuel is therefore an ongoing institutional and intergenerational undertaking, distinct from the possible recycling of construction concrete (NWMO 2026). The proposed LC³ mixes do not imply a proven advantage in recyclability or radiation clearance relative to OPC concrete. Their demonstrated benefit in the current comparison is a reduction in production-related embodied greenhouse-gas emissions. Any claims about reduced demolition waste, greater structural life or lower radioactive waste generation would require separate evidence.

### 3.6.5 Australia: statutory restrictions and the

Liddell counterfactual A BWRX-300 at Liddell remains hypothetical under current Australian law. Section 140A of the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (Cth) prevents the federal Environment Minister from approving specified nuclear installations, including nuclear power plants. Section 10 of the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Act 1998 imposes restrictions on relevant Commonwealth licensing. Section 8 of the Uranium Mining and Nuclear Facilities (Prohibitions) Act 1986 (NSW) restricts nuclear facility construction and operation within NSW, subject to provisions in the statute (Commonwealth of Australia 2026a, 2026b; NSW Government 2025). Legal change alone would not create an immediately deployable commercial project. Australia would require an appropriate reactor licensing and monitoring framework, trained inspectors, emergency arrangements, spent-fuel stewardship provisions, procurement capacity, site-specific environmental assessment, consultation and an economic case that considers system-level alternatives. Liddell is also undergoing other energy redevelopment. Consequently, estimates based on today's average NSW grid emissions should not be presented as future project savings without a commissioning date and a credible model of the generation that would actually be displaced. CSIRO's 2025–26 analysis finds that wind and solar supported by storage remain central to Australia's lowest-cost net-zero electricity pathway. This does not establish that nuclear energy can never be advantageous in any circumstance, but it places a burden on any Australian SMR proposal to demonstrate competitive whole-system cost, feasible delivery timing and additional decarbonisation benefits (CSIRO 2026). None of these first-order barriers can be solved by changing Portland cement to LC³.

### 3.6.6 Regulatory and sustainability synthesis

The regulatory comparison demonstrates different kinds of feasibility challenges. In Ontario, an existing project and reactor regulator enable a materials innovation to be evaluated through established procurement, testing and configuration-control systems, although future units and safety-class material substitutions still require authorisation. In NSW, the main barrier precedes detailed materials selection: civilian nuclear power faces statutory restrictions and an extensive new institutional and delivery requirement. The environmental preference for LC³ is therefore conditional on its application, supply chain and the absence of negative safety or schedule effects. Independent engineering assurance and public accountability are not obstacles separate from sustainability; they are necessary safeguards against transferring embodied-carbon reductions into structural, occupational or intergenerational risks. The proposed material intervention is justified as a controlled innovation within a credible nuclear construction programme, not as a general argument that all SMRs should be constructed or that nuclear generation must replace Australia's lowest-cost alternatives.
