# Abstract
Small modular reactors (SMRs) are proposed to replace closing coal plants. This report asks whether an SMR is feasible across the triple bottom line, and whether a materials choice can cut its footprint without delaying it. We study the GE Vernova Hitachi BWRX-300 (300 MWe) at Darlington, Ontario, and compare Liddell, New South Wales, a closed coal site.

Our initiative, C1, is low-clinker concrete for the steel-composite infill and forebay shaft of Darlington Units 2–4: pilot limestone calcined clay cement (LC³) in eligible non-safety works first, extend it to the infill only if qualified, and adopt it only if schedule-neutral. A screening life-cycle assessment (ISO 14040/44 principles) gives about 4 g CO₂e/kWh for one unit, mostly from the fuel front end. C1 saves about 2,200 t per unit (1,100–3,300), about 0.36% of the life cycle, and a delay of about 12–18 days cancels it.

Law, cost and timing decide the Australian case. An SMR at Liddell could not start before about 2040, and firmed renewables avoid 10.9 times more CO₂e per dollar. In a multi-criteria analysis, renewables rank first under all four weight sets.

We recommend piloting LC³ in eligible non-safety works first, and extending it to the infill only if qualified on Unit 1 mock-ups. In Australia, we recommend firmed renewables.

# 1 Introduction

Small modular reactors (SMRs) are one proposed replacement for closing coal plants. We ask whether an SMR is feasible across the triple bottom line (environmental, economic, social), and whether a materials choice can make it cleaner without delaying it.

Liddell in New South Wales (NSW) has closed (Australian Energy Regulator, 2021), yet NSW's grid still emits about 640 g CO₂e/kWh (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water [DCCEEW], 2025); Ontario's emits about 74 (The Atmospheric Fund, 2025). An SMR is a reactor of up to about 300 MWe built largely from factory-made modules (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2024).

*Darlington, Ontario* is the initiative site: Ontario Power Generation (OPG) plans four BWRX-300 units for C$20.9 billion, Unit 1 in service in 2030 (Ontario Power Generation, 2025a). *Liddell* is the Australian comparison.

OPG should pilot limestone calcined clay cement (LC³) in eligible non-safety works first, using it in the steel-composite infill only if qualified and schedule-neutral (Sections 3.1.1–3.1.4, Section 3.1.5).

We answer four research questions.

1. What does one BWRX-300 emit? (Section 3.2)
2. How much does C1 save, and what delay cancels it? (Sections 3.2–3.3)
3. Does a unit's climate value differ between Ontario and Liddell? (Section 3.3)
4. Which barriers decide, and does an SMR beat firmed renewables? (Section 3.1.3, Sections 3.4–3.7)


# 2 Data Sources/Methodology

[LEROY: once Granta is run, this section's Granta sentence changes to describe it.]{custom-style="TeamNote"}


Four methods carry the numbers: a screening life-cycle assessment (LCA), grid displacement with a schedule break-even, a concentration index for fuel supply, and a multi-criteria analysis. Scripts and parameter files regenerate every figure (Appendix A1). Values come from the documents cited; our assumptions and inferences are labelled.

A screening study following ISO 14040/14044 principles (International Organization for Standardization, 2006a; 2006b), not critically reviewed: functional unit 1 MWh of net electricity at the grid connection, cradle to grave (Section 3.2.1). A Granta EduPack cross-check was planned but not completed.

One unit (300 MWe net assumed) supplies 2.365 TWh a year and avoids that output × (grid factor − SMR factor). The *average* factor is the grid's mean intensity; the *marginal* factor is that of the plant displaced (The Atmospheric Fund, 2025). Ontario's are 73.8 and 343 g/kWh; NSW uses 640 (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water [DCCEEW], 2025). Start years are 2030 at Darlington (Ontario Power Generation, 2025a) and 2040 at the earliest in Australia (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024). The *schedule break-even* is the delay that cancels a saving S: S ÷ (daily output × grid-factor gap) (Appendix B4–B5).

The Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) sums squared percentage market shares (0 to 10,000); above 1,800 is highly concentrated (US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission, 2023). Capacity is not contracts (Appendix B2).

A weighted sum (Dodgson et al., 2009) scores four options (status quo, one SMR at Liddell, the same SMR built with C1's concrete, firmed renewables) on seven criteria, each min-max normalised. Four weight sets are our judgement; robustness is tested in Appendix B7.

Search strategy and patent search: Appendix E.

Cost, not licensing, stopped NuScale. Its design was approved in May 2025, yet the first plant was cancelled when the estimate reached US$9.24 bn as reported, about US$20,000/kW (NuScale Power Corporation, 2023). Darlington's C$20.9 bn for 1,200 MW is C$17,400/kW (Ontario Power Generation, 2025a; 2025b); both are first-of-a-kind, in different currencies. Darlington's construction licence took about 29 months and Clinch River's permit 14 (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, 2025).

Appendix E, from search summaries only, found GE-Hitachi isolation-condenser patents but none on the diaphragm-plate composite modules, and no core LC³ composition patent, though Sika and Construction Research & Technology hold LC³ applications. This suggests, but does not establish, that C1's binder risk lies in qualification rather than patents; no freedom-to-operate search was done.

Australia's earliest date is about 2040. The Coalition's 2024 plan claimed SMR power by 2035 (ABC News, 2024); CSIRO's GenCost 2023-24 is reported to say 2040 at the earliest (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024). Darlington supports CSIRO: even with a licensed site, it needs about eight years from licence application (October 2022) to power (October 2030) (Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, 2026a; Ontario Power Generation, 2025b). Australia must first repeal two federal bans (Section 3.6.1), assumed by 2029, and build a licensing regime, assumed to take three years: 2029 + 3 + 8 gives about 2040 (Section 3.3).


### 3.1.2 Technical Descriptions

The BWRX-300's composite building is what C1 targets. The reactor is rated 300 MWe (GE Vernova Hitachi Nuclear Energy, n.d.; 315 MW net in Ontario Power Generation, 2025b), with a 60-year design life (Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, 2024). The vendor-reported capacity factor is 95% (Fermi Energia, n.d.); we use 0.90. Passive isolation condensers cool the core for seven days (GE Hitachi Nuclear Energy, n.d.). The reactor building sits in a below-grade shaft of diaphragm-plate steel-composite (DP-SC) modules filled with self-consolidating concrete (SCC) (GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024).

The vendor's concrete claim is hard to reconcile with Ostrołęka. GE Vernova (n.d.) claims about half the concrete per MW of its larger ESBWR, yet Ostrołęka's information sheet gives 50,000 m³ per unit, or 167 m³/MWe (BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023), against the ESBWR's 69 (Peterson, Zhao and Petroski, 2005). The gap probably reflects the boundary (our inference); a small reactor does not mean small concrete (Section 3.1.4).

### 3.1.4 The Proposed Initiative

C1 targets a materials problem that modular construction creates, and it is worth doing only if it is schedule-neutral. The BWRX-300's reactor building is made of diaphragm-plate steel-composite (DPSC) modules filled on site with concrete. The infill must flow between closely spaced plates, so we assume a cement-rich self-consolidating mix (our inference; GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024). A delay of 11.6–18.5 days to one unit on Ontario's average grid, or 1.7–1.8 days on its marginal grid, cancels that unit's saving (Section 3.3.1).

Ontario Power Generation (OPG) should pilot limestone calcined clay cement (LC³; Scrivener et al., 2018) in eligible non-safety works first, and use it in the infill only if qualified on Unit 1 mock-ups; Units 2–4 need future licensing (Section 3.6.1, Section 3.6.2). The Ostrołęka BWRX-300 design uses 50,000 m³ of concrete per unit (BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023). If 20%, 40% or 60% is eligible, replacing ordinary cement with LC³ (380 to 270 kg CO₂e/m³; Pillai et al., 2019) saves 1,100, 2,200 or 3,300 t CO₂e per unit (40% central), and 6,600 t across Units 2–4. A qualified slag blend is the fallback.

Why it is not the obvious choice. C1's value is set by schedule, not by the concrete: under three weeks of delay to a unit cancels its saving (Section 3.3.1), so C1 is a qualification programme timed to Unit 1 mock-ups. Its lever is financial: the Units 2–4 equity tranche can carry the condition (Section 3.6.2).

What C1 does not address. Cost, law, fuel supply, social licence and workforce (Table T2); lower-carbon steel is the next lever (Section 3.2.3, Section 3.8).

One BWRX-300 at Darlington emits an estimated 4.4 g CO₂e per kWh (Monte Carlo median; 90% interval 3.2–6.1; 3.5 if conversion heat takes its central published value), below UNECE's 5.1–6.4 g/kWh for nuclear power. ### 3.2.1 Goal, Scope and Method

This is a screening LCA of one BWRX-300 at Darlington following ISO 14040/14044 principles (International Organization for Standardization, 2006a; 2006b), not critically reviewed.

Functional unit: 1 MWh of net electricity at the grid connection. One unit delivers 300 MWe × 8,760 h × 0.90 × 60 years = 1.419 × 10⁸ MWh (Appendix A2). Boundary (Figure F1): cradle to grave. Construction materials and the fuel front end are modelled from published factors; operation and maintenance (O&M), decommissioning and the back end are per-kWh values from EDF's French-fleet study (EDF R&D, 2024). Baseline (S0): the concrete OPG would pour anyway, at 380 kg CO₂e/m³ (Pillai et al., 2019, as reported). We have not seen OPG's mix or any cement-type limit (GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024). Cut-offs: fuel cladding, conversion chemicals, upstream gas, fuel-plant capital goods and recycling credits; each biases the result low (Section 3.2.5). Impact category: GWP100.

A reproducible Python model multiplies quantities by published emission factors (Appendices A and C). Plant quantities come from the Ostrołęka project information sheet (BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023, as reported): 50,000 m³ of concrete and 14,000 t of steel per unit, a design proxy, not confirmed Darlington quantities. A bottom-up scaling (20,700 m³; Peterson, Zhao and Petroski, 2005) is a cross-check (Appendix A3). A Granta EduPack Eco Audit was planned but not completed; no number here comes from Granta.

### 3.2.2 Baseline and Hotspots

One unit emits 4.40 g/kWh (median; 90% interval 3.24–6.05). With every input at its most likely value (the "mode case") it is 2.86; Section 3.2.4 explains the gap. On the expected basis (Figure F2):

Mining and milling: 1.26 g/kWh (28%), low because Saskatchewan ores are high-grade (Parker, McNaughton and Sparrow, 2016). Conversion: 1.10 g/kWh (25%), an *uncertainty* hotspot: published heat demand spans 26–665 kWh per kg of uranium (Gibon and Hahn Menacho, 2023); at the most likely value it is only 0.13 g/kWh. Back end: 0.65 g/kWh (15%), transferred from France, which reprocesses; Canada plans direct disposal (Nuclear Waste Management Organization, 2024a), so this may be high.

The back end and decommissioning add 18%, O&M 9% and construction 11.5% (0.52 g/kWh). The C1-eligible concrete is only 1.9% of the mode-case total, so C1 acts on about one-fiftieth of the footprint. Ostrołęka's 167 m³ per MWe is 2.4 times the ESBWR's 69 (Peterson, Zhao and Petroski, 2005), so scale effects outweigh the vendor's claim of 50% less concrete per unit of energy (GE Vernova, n.d.). The cross-check gives 4.20 g/kWh (Appendix A8).

### 3.2.3 The Initiative against the Baseline

C1 is low-clinker concrete: LC³ on eligible non-safety works first and on the steel-composite infill only if qualified, a slag blend as the fallback, specified only if schedule-neutral (Section 3.1.4). Pillai et al. (2019, as reported) give 380 kg CO₂e/m³ for ordinary concrete and 270 for LC³, a cut of 110 kg/m³ (28.9%). The eligible share is our scenario, not data. At 20, 40 and 60% of 50,000 m³, C1 saves 1,100, 2,200 and 3,300 t per unit, or 0.18, 0.36 and 0.54% of its life cycle (Table T5). Varying eligibility, the cut and volumes together gives 2,217 t (1,375–3,294). Only Units 2–4 count, because Unit 1's basemat is placed.

Four findings follow.

The saving is small at plant scale: about 0.016 g/kWh, so C1 does not change the plant's climate case (economic value: Section 3.5.1). Slag is about as good as LC³, so the case is for low-clinker concrete in general. LC³ beats slag in only 55% of draws (median difference +54 t; 90% interval −588 to +766). The specification should accept whichever binder qualifies first (Section 3.6.1). Lower-carbon steel is the larger lever but is not part of C1: all 14,000 t at best-practice electric-arc-furnace factors would save up to 3,915 t (1.8 times LC³), an upper bound not shown feasible for nuclear-grade plate (Appendix A7; Section 3.8). Schedule dominates. A delay of 11.6–18.5 days to one unit on Ontario's average grid factor, or 1.7–1.8 days on the marginal factor, cancels its saving (Section 3.3.1; Appendix B5). C1 is specified only if schedule-neutral (Section 3.6.2).

### 3.2.5 Comparison with Published Values

Our median (3.5–4.4 across conversion-heat assumptions) is below UNECE's 5.1–6.4 g/kWh (United Nations Economic Commission for Europe, 2021), though our 90% interval overlaps it, and sits at the low end of the wider literature for reasons we can identify (Figure F5). It overlaps EDF's French fleet (3.7; EDF R&D, 2024) and is below the global average of 6.1 (Gibon and Hahn Menacho, 2023), an SMR estimate of 9.1 (Carless, Griffin and Fischbeck, 2016) and the harmonised and IPCC AR5 medians of 12 (Warner and Heath, 2012; Schlömer et al., 2014). Gas combined cycle (403–513 g/kWh) and coal (751–1,095) are 90–120 and 170–250 times higher (United Nations Economic Commission for Europe, 2021).

Three reasons explain most of the gap (Appendix C7): Ore grade: our mining is 1.0–1.3 g/kWh, against about 2.8 at global-average grades (Gibon and Hahn Menacho, 2023). Enrichment: centrifuges at 50 kWh per separative work unit, against about 2,500 for older diffusion plants. Cut-offs and long life: a 60-year life biases the result low; at 40 years the mode case rises from 2.86 to 3.07 g/kWh.

The known low bias would raise the total and shrink C1's share, so it does not weaken Section 3.2.3.

## 3.3 National Strategies and Industrial Policies

For C1, schedule matters far more than concrete. A one-month slip of Units 2–4 costs C$33–110 m in carrying cost alone, 29–175 times the carbon value of C1's whole saving, and defers about C$88 m of output. C1's 2,200 t per unit (1,100–3,300; Section 3.2.3) is cancelled by 11.6–18.5 days of delay on Ontario's average grid factor, or 1.7–1.8 days on its marginal factor. ### 3.3.2 Carbon Displaced: Darlington vs Liddell

At today's grid factors, a unit at Liddell would avoid about nine times more CO₂e than one at Darlington. Timing closes most of that gap. One unit avoids 1.50 Mt a year on the NSW average of 640 g/kWh (DCCEEW, 2025). On Ontario's 2024 average of 73.8 g/kWh it avoids 0.165 Mt (9.1× less), and on its marginal factor of 343 g/kWh 0.80 Mt (1.9× less) (The Atmospheric Fund, 2025).

The plants would not run in the same years. Darlington Unit 1 is planned for 2030 (Ontario Power Generation, 2025a); Australia's earliest date for nuclear is 2040 (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024), against the Coalition's 2035 as reported (ABC News, 2024). If coal leaves the NEM by 2038 (Australian Energy Market Operator, 2024), a 2040 unit displaces only a residual 30 g/kWh (assumed).

To 2050 (Figure F6), Darlington Unit 1 avoids 1.0–6.8 Mt and Liddell 0.67–3.9 Mt; Liddell wins clearly only if the NEM stays at 640 g/kWh (16.5 Mt). Grid trajectory, start year and post-coal floor move these results tenfold; capacity factor and our life-cycle interval move them by a few per cent (Appendix B4). An SMR's climate value depends on when it runs and what it displaces, not on today's coal share.

### 3.3.3 Firmed Renewables for the Same Money

At GenCost's 2030 costs, the money for one SMR unit would buy about 2.9 times as much energy from firmed renewables, which would avoid about 11 times more CO₂e to 2050 at Liddell.

GenCost 2023-24 gives A$230–382/MWh for an SMR in 2030, against A$89–125 for solar and wind with integration costs (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024, as reported). With renewables from 2028 (assumed) and the SMR from 2040, renewables avoid 10.9× more CO₂e to 2050. They still avoid 5.7× more in the worst case we tested for them (SMR A$230, renewables A$153), and 4.9× with the SMR at A$198 (Appendix B6, Table B28). Renewables win because they are cheaper per MWh and start about a decade earlier; integration costs rise beyond the shares GenCost models. On carbon per dollar, an SMR at Liddell loses. ### 3.4.2 Social Licence

The same reactor has a social licence at Darlington and none at Liddell. Safety data do not explain the difference; consent, benefit and trust do (Table T8).

In 2024, 61% of Australians supported using nuclear power to generate electricity (Neelam, 2024), yet 59% wanted to keep the legal ban, up from 51% a year earlier (Griffith University Climate Action Beacon and Monash Climate Change Communication Research Hub, 2025). One poll asks about a technology and the other about the law, so support depends on framing. The health benefit, like the carbon benefit (Section 3.3), depends on what the reactor displaces.

Consent: equity in Canada, failed top-down siting in Australia. At Darlington the CNSC found the duty to consult met (CNSC, 2025). In June 2026 the seven Williams Treaties First Nations announced a C$700 m investment, a loan converting to equity once all four units are complete, backed by federal and Ontario loan guarantees of about C$715 m (Williams Treaties First Nations et al., 2026; Government of Canada, 2026). Two of the seven told the CNSC in 2023–24 that their concerns had not been addressed (Mississaugas of Scugog Island First Nation, 2023; Curve Lake First Nation, 2024), so ownership is not universal consent. Australia's precedent is a waste site chosen top-down and set aside by the Federal Court for apprehended bias (*Barngarla Determination Aboriginal Corporation RNTBC v Minister for Resources* [2023] FCA 809); Canada's waste agency used volunteer siting and a priced hosting agreement (Nuclear Waste Management Organization [NWMO], 2024a). Scored on legitimacy, legal risk, speed, public cost and fairness, consent-based routes beat top-down siting by 3–6 points under every weighting, and not building scores highest (Appendix B8.3).

C1 changes no land use, emissions or waste at the site, so it raises no new consent issue there; its social value is indirect, as a lower-carbon build that OPG can report to its First Nations and public equity partners (our inference). A socially sustainable pilot would disclose clay source locations and routes early, open supply to Indigenous and local firms where suitable, and report environmental data plainly. Waste stewardship is unchanged: the NWMO repository near Ignace entered regulatory assessment in January 2026 and is not operating (Nuclear Waste Management Organization, 2026). For Liddell, low-regret steps can precede any repeal: a producer-funded waste body and a two-key test in which both the host community and the Traditional Owners must agree, then a priced hosting agreement, First Nations equity with a loan guarantee and a skills plan coordinated with AUKUS (Section 3.6.2).

## 3.5 Market Analysis and Economic Feasibility

### 3.5.1 Economics, Finance and Market Barriers

An SMR's cost is mostly capital, so whoever finances it sets its price and decides who can build one. C1 is the opposite: it costs almost nothing, but no one is paid for the carbon it saves.

Darlington's C$20.9 bn budget for four 300 MWe units (Ontario Power Generation, 2025a) gives a levelised cost of electricity (LCOE: the constant price that repays every cost over the plant's life) of C$198/MWh at a 7% real discount rate, and C$112–280 from 3% to 10% (Table T10). Capital is 65–86% of it, so finance terms matter as much as engineering. OPG's C$149/MWh implies 4.9%, a regulated Crown utility's cost of capital. Our A$221 at 7% sits just below GenCost's 2030 SMR range (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024), though GenCost's current edition bases its SMR capital cost on Darlington (Graham and Hayward, 2026), so the agreement is not independent. A NuScale-size overrun would give C$317/MWh.

NuScale's Idaho project shows what happens when buyers carry overrun risk: its price rose about 35% in real terms even after tax credits, the Utah utilities did not subscribe, and it was cancelled in 2023 (Table T10). Darlington avoids this with a single Crown owner and a regulated rate base; public and First Nations finance covers about 22% of the budget (Section 3.4.1). The Canada Growth Fund (up to C$2 bn) and Building Ontario Fund (up to C$1 bn) supply equity, and the C$715 m of First Nations loan guarantees is credit support, not extra spending (Government of Canada, 2026). The second equity tranche is released for Units 2–4 "once project milestones are met" (Canada Growth Fund and Building Ontario Fund, 2025), which is C1's main financial lever.

Entry needs large up-front capital, years of development, component qualification and regulatory approval; a small module is not proportionally cheap because site works, security and licensing are largely fixed (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2023). About C$17,400/kW is at risk for years before any revenue. First plants are financed by states and rate bases: the US Department of Energy's US$800 m went to two designs, one the BWRX-300 (US Department of Energy, 2025), out of about 70 worldwide (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2024), and China's State Council approves batches for state-owned builders, as reported (Caixin Global, 2026). Australia has neither route. Liddell belongs to AGL, a private company, so an SMR there would need Commonwealth ownership or underwriting, with taxpayers carrying the cost risk (our inference).

LC³ saves 6,600 t CO₂e across Units 2–4 (2,200 t a unit; Section 3.2.3), worth C$0.63–0.76 m at the federal carbon price of C$95/t in 2026 and C$115/t in 2030 (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2026; BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023; Pillai et al., 2019). That justifies an LC³ premium of up to C$10.4–12.7/m³, or 4.6–5.5% of commodity concrete at C$229/m³. The target concrete costs about C$13.7 m, 0.10% of Units 2–4 capital. Table T9 tests the break-even from the cost side: a premium on a unit's 20,000 m³ of eligible concrete (40% of 50,000 m³) is sensitivity, not a quote, since no supplier price has been obtained. Qualification adds to the abatement cost: a C$1 m programme (assumed) adds about C$152/t, and one day of delay costs C$1.8–2.9 m, more than C1's whole carbon value. Responses are compared in Section 3.6.2 (Table T13).

### 3.5.2 Supply Concentration and Geopolitics

Every front-end fuel stage we measured is concentrated: each Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI; Section 2) exceeds 1,800, "highly concentrated" on the 2023 US merger guidelines (US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission, 2023), though on the 2010 scale only enrichment is (Table T11). For Darlington this is a price risk; for an Australian SMR it is a sovereign risk.

Russia holds 41.0–42.8% of enrichment capacity (calculated from World Nuclear Association, 2025; World Nuclear University, 2025). Removing Russia, as US buyers must once import-ban waivers end in 2028 (United States Congress, 2024), raises every HHI; without Russia and China, enrichment is a Urenco–Orano duopoly (HHI 5,621). Mining is concentrated by country but not by company (HHI about 850–1,400), so its risk is geopolitical (Appendix B2).

OPG's announced Unit 1 fuel suppliers (Cameco, Urenco USA, Orano, GNF-A) include no Russian firm (Ontario Power Generation, 2023, as reported by Power Engineering, 2023). Four units need about 196 t of natural uranium and 138 thousand separative work units (SWU) a year, about 3.2% of Urenco USA's output (assumed typical BWR fuel values; Appendix B2.6). Australia holds 28% of identified recoverable uranium resources (OECD Nuclear Energy Agency and International Atomic Energy Agency, 2025; the 2026 edition gives 27–29%) but has no conversion or enrichment (World Nuclear Association, 2026), so an Australian SMR would import enrichment and fabrication by law (EPBC s 140A; ARPANS s 10) and conversion (Parliamentary Library, 2024).

C1 does not reduce fuel risk, and fuel independence needs conversion, enrichment, fabrication, forged components and skilled labour, not uranium deposits alone. Contracts with allied enrichers, as OPG uses, cut a buyer's exposure but not concentration (Section 3.6.2). C1 adds a small supply risk of its own: LC³ needs a suitable calcined-clay source and blending capacity (Scrivener et al., 2018), and a single qualified supplier would be a risk. One Western option is laser enrichment from Australia's Silex Systems, commercialised in the US by Global Laser Enrichment (51% Silex, 49% Cameco, OPG's uranium supplier) (Silex Systems Limited, 2025; 2026, as reported by World Nuclear News, 2026); EPBC s 140A would bar such a plant in Australia (Section 3.8).

## 3.6 Government Regulations Review

### 3.6.1 Law, Regulation and Licensing

Law is where Darlington and Liddell differ most. At Darlington, C1 starts with LC³ in non-safety concrete and extends to the steel-composite infill only if qualified before the Units 2–4 go-ahead expected in 2028 (American Nuclear Society, 2026, as reported). At Liddell, three laws and the site owner block an SMR (Table T12).

Federal repeal fails in the Senate while Labor and the Greens oppose, so the Coalition's pledge to "repeal all legislative prohibitions" (Liberal Party of Australia, 2026) cannot pass unless one changes position. Repeal alone would not suffice: ARPANSA licenses only Commonwealth-controlled bodies (ARPANS Act s 13), so a private SMR at Liddell would remain an offence under NSW s 8. Only a Commonwealth-owned operator might fit the s 8(3) exemption that covers ANSTO (our inference).

The construction licence (April 2025) covers one unit, and OPG applied in March 2026 to operate Unit 1 (Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission [CNSC], 2026a; 2026b). Units 2–4 need a further authorisation, with C1 reviewed inside it. Concrete in a non-safety auxiliary structure meets ordinary construction requirements, but infill in a safety-significant structure may alter the licensed design basis and needs a fuller demonstration; REGDOC-2.5.2 classifies structures by risk (CNSC, 2023a). The cement standard already allows calcined-clay blends with up to 15% limestone (CSA Group, 2023; Hooton, 2023). The BWRX-300 containment is steel-plate composite, so the infill is governed by GE-Hitachi's topical report NEDO-33926, which the CNSC and US NRC jointly reviewed (GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024; US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and CNSC, 2025). To our reading the public report does not state a cement-type limit, and we found no nuclear precedent for calcined-clay infill: that is C1's real licensing risk.

The non-safety pilot needs no nuclear qualification; infill qualification must start in 2027, with slag (S1) as the fallback (Section 3.6.2). At Liddell the barrier precedes any materials choice, and LC³ solves none of them.

The steps total about 18 to 24 months (assumed), so they fit only if they start in 2027. We have not costed the programme; Section 3.7 tests an assumed C$1 m. If the evidence is not ready, Units 2–4 proceed with the current mix and the infill enters at a later unit. Doing nothing forgoes the saving at near-zero cost and is rejected.

The federal standard is a template, not an obligation. The Standard on Embodied Carbon in Construction (amended March 2025) requires low-carbon concrete and life-cycle assessment in federal procurement (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, 2022), but it binds federal departments, and OPG, an Ontario Crown corporation, is outside its scope (our inference). Its allowance of 130% of baseline for specialised mixes could let self-consolidating infill emit 30% more than the baseline, so any OPG or fund clause must benchmark infill against a cement-rich self-consolidating reference mix.

### 3.7.3 Verdict and Limits

C1 is justified as a controlled innovation within a credible nuclear construction programme, not as an argument that all SMRs should be built. Pilot LC³ in eligible non-safety works first; use steel-composite infill only if qualified on Unit 1 mock-ups, otherwise the slag blend. Qualification must start in 2027 for the 2028 Units 2–4 decision (Section 3.6.2).

An SMR at Liddell would need its cost gap closed, first power well before 2040, and the legal and consent barriers removed (Appendix B3, route C). We would keep SMRs under review against Darlington's outturn cost.

The social, legal and supply scores and all four weight sets are our judgement; only "renewables first" is robust to the scaling. Costs are GenCost's (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024); the renewables supply score uses solar PV alone (International Energy Agency, 2022).

# 4 Conclusion

Pilot LC³ in eligible non-safety works first; use it in the infill only if qualified on Unit 1 mock-ups and adding no delay to Darlington Units 2–4. For Australia, law, cost and timing bar an SMR at Liddell before about 2040; firmed renewables rank first.

Our answers to the four research questions are as follows.

1. *Life cycle (Section 3.2).* One BWRX-300 emits about 4 g CO₂e/kWh, mostly from the fuel front end; conversion heat is the largest uncertainty.
2. *C1 (Sections 3.2–3.3).* It saves 2,200 t per unit (1,100–3,300; Section 3.1.4), about 0.36% of the life cycle; a delay of 11.6–18.5 days cancels it (1.7–1.8 days on the marginal grid factor).
3. *Displacement (Section 3.3).* Darlington Unit 1 avoids 1.0–6.8 Mt CO₂e to 2050 and a Liddell unit 0.67–3.9 Mt; firmed renewables avoid 10.9 times more per dollar.
4. *Barriers and options (Section 3.1.3, Sections 3.4–3.7).* Law (three bans), cost (C$149–198/MWh) and time (2040 at the earliest) decide; firmed renewables rank first under all four weight sets (80 against 30 with equal weights).

Schedule: a month's slip to Units 2–4 costs 29–175 times C1's carbon value (Section 3.3). Fuel front end: a measured conversion-heat value would narrow the headline most (Section 3.2). Whole-plant steel: up to 3,714 t per unit, if feasible (Section 3.8). Concrete (C1): low-risk, but moves the life cycle by about 0.36%.

A screening LCA, not critically reviewed; MCDA weights are our judgement; legislation excludes the 2025 EPBC reforms.

## Appendix B. Policy, economics and supply calculations

#### B1 Legal and political pathway

Senate arithmetic (Parliament of Australia, 2026; 76 seats):
- Majority = ⌊76/2⌋ + 1 = **39**.
- Largest pro-repeal bloc = Coalition 27 + One Nation 4 + Babet 1 = 32 (the last two are assumptions); shortfall = 39 − 32 = **7**.
- Adding every other crossbencher gives 36–37, still short. Labor 30 + Greens 10 = 40 blocks.

NSW repeal bill: introduced 19 November 2025, passed the Legislative Council 6 May 2026, stalled in the Assembly, lapses 28 November 2026.

Darlington licensing (Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, 2021; 2025; 2026a; 2026b): application 31 October 2022 to licence to construct 4 April 2025 = 886 days ÷ 30.44 = **29.1 months**; environmental assessment start (May 2007) to licence = **17.9 years**.

#### B4 Displacement
Formulae: E = 300 MW × 8,760 h × 0.90 = 2,365,200 MWh/yr; avoided in year y = E × (EF_grid − EF_SMR) / 10⁶ t, with EF_SMR = 4.2 g/kWh (our Monte Carlo median).
- Static NSW grid (640 g/kWh; Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2025): 2,365,200,000 × (640 − 4.2) / 10⁶ = **1.50 Mt a year**.
- Static Ontario average 2024 (73.8 g/kWh; The Atmospheric Fund, 2025): 0.165 Mt a year; marginal (343 g/kWh): 0.80 Mt.
- Cumulative to 2050: Darlington Unit 1 from 2030 = **1.01–6.80 Mt**; Liddell from 2040 = **0.67–3.85 Mt** (Step Change proxy to slow transition); frozen NEM = 16.54 Mt.
- Liddell example: 2,365,200,000 × (30 − 4.2) / 10⁶ = 61,022 t a year; × 11 years = 671 kt.

Grid trajectory, start year and post-coal floor dominate; the SMR's own life-cycle value moves the result by a few per cent.

#### B5 Value of schedule
Formulae: daily output D = E / 365 = 6,480,000 kWh; gross loss per day = D × (EF_grid − EF_SMR) / 10⁶; break-even days = saving / loss per day.
- Ontario average (2035 factor 33.4 g/kWh): 189 t a day, so C1's 2,200 t is cancelled by **11.6 days** (net basis 18.5).
- Ontario marginal declining: 1,296 t a day, **1.7 days**.
- Units 2–4 saving (6,600 t) against delay to one unit: 34.9 days (average), 5.1 days (marginal).
- Carbon value of C1, Units 2–4: 6,600 t × C$95/t = C$0.63 m (C$0.76 m at C$115; Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2026).
- One-month slip of Units 2–4: deferred output 591,300 MWh × C$149 = C$88 m (Ontario Power Generation, 2026b); carrying cost on C$13.2 bn at 3–10% = C$33–110 m. Ratio to C1's carbon value: **29–175 times**.

#### B6 Same money
Formulae: CRF = r(1 + r)ⁿ / ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1) = 0.0806 (7%, 30 years); energy ratio = SMR LCOE / renewables LCOE.
- Inputs (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024, as reported): SMR A$306/MWh (230–382), firmed renewables A$107/MWh (89–125), 2030; Darlington C$20.9 bn (Government of Ontario, 2025) at A$1.119 per C$.
- Energy ratio = 306 / 107 = **2.9×**; the same annual spend (A$0.72 bn) buys 6.8 TWh against 2.37 TWh.
- Capital only: C$5.225 bn × 1.119 × 0.0806 = A$471 m a year ÷ 2,365,200 MWh = **A$199/MWh**, buying 1.9× the energy.
- CO₂e avoided to 2050, renewables ÷ SMR, Liddell from 2040: 5.7× (frozen grid), 10.9× (slow transition), 26.0× (Step Change); range across cases tested 4.9–26.0×.
- Ontario average grid: SMR wins (−0.4×), because the grid falls below the renewables' assumed 30 g/kWh.

#### B7 Multi-criteria analysis
Method (Dodgson et al., 2009): min–max normalisation s = (x − worst) / (best − worst); total T = 100 × Σ w_c s_c. Seven criteria: life-cycle CO₂e, CO₂e avoided per dollar, cost, first-power year, supply HHI, social licence, legal feasibility (last two judgement).

Worked example (SMR, equal weights): 0.99998 + 0.0921 + 0.0001 + 0.4545 + 0.5298 + 0 + 0 = 2.0766; T = 100 × 2.0766 / 7 = **29.7**.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| W0 equal | 52.4 | 29.7 | 29.7 | 80.1 |
| W1 climate and cost | 41.7 | 24.0 | 24.0 | 85.9 |
| W2 firm and sovereign | 60.0 | 43.6 | 43.6 | 63.7 |
| W3 host community | 55.0 | 20.4 | 20.4 | 89.3 |

Robustness: ±50% weight tests change first place in 2 of 56 cases (both in W2); under 20,000 random weight sets renewables rank first in 85.4%. C1 changes the SMR's life cycle from 4.200 to 4.1845 g/kWh (2,200 / 1.419 × 10¹¹ kWh = 0.0155 g/kWh), under 0.005 point of score.

#### C5 Why the median (4.40) sits above the mode case (2.86)
Skewed upper tails, mainly conversion heat: triangular mean (26 + 26 + 665) / 3 ≈ 239 kWh/kg U, nine times the mode, adds +0.98 of the +1.62 g/kWh gap. With conversion heat fixed at 26 the median is 3.51; log-uniform 26–665 gives 4.11. The reported range of medians is **3.51–4.40 g CO₂e/kWh**.
