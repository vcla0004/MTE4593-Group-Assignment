## 8.1 Law, regulation and licensing

Law is where Darlington and Liddell differ most. At Darlington, C1 starts with LC³ in non-safety concrete and extends to the steel-composite infill only if qualified before the Units 2–4 go-ahead expected in 2028 (American Nuclear Society, 2026, as reported). At Liddell, three laws and the site owner block an SMR (Table T7).

**Australia.** Federal repeal fails in the Senate while Labor and the Greens oppose, so the Coalition's pledge to "repeal all legislative prohibitions" (Liberal Party of Australia, 2026) cannot pass unless one of them changes position. Repeal alone would not be enough: ARPANSA licenses only Commonwealth-controlled bodies (ARPANS Act s 13), so a private SMR at Liddell would remain an offence under NSW s 8. Only a Commonwealth-owned operator might fit the s 8(3) exemption that covers ANSTO (our inference).

**Canada.** The construction licence to construct (April 2025) covers one unit, so unit. The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) reports that its first construction hold point was removed in March 2026, and OPG applied that month to operate Unit 1 (CNSC, 2026a; 2026b). Units 2–4 are not covered by the first licence, so they need a further CNSC authorisation, with C1 reviewed inside it (our inference). it. The review depends on safety class. Concrete in a non-safety auxiliary structure meets ordinary construction requirements, but infill in a safety-significant structure may alter the licensed design basis and needs a fuller demonstration. REGDOC-2.5.2 (version 2.1) sets risk-informed design requirements and classifies structures (CNSC, 2023a), so no containment standard should be applied to every structure. The cement standard already allows calcined-clay blends with up to 15% limestone (CSA Group, 2023; Hooton, 2023). The infill, though, BWRX-300 containment is steel-plate composite, so the infill is governed by GE-Hitachi's topical report NEDO-33926 (self-consolidating concrete, strength per draft AISC N690, not CSA N287), N690), which the CNSC and US NRC jointly reviewed (GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024; US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and CNSC, 2025). CSA N287 applies only to concrete containment, where applicable (CSA Group, 2026). To our reading the public report does not state a cement-type limit, and we found no nuclear precedent for calcined-clay infill: that is C1's real licensing risk. A lower carbon figure cannot override the acceptance criteria for strength, crack control or design life.

**Translation to practice.** The non-safety pilot needs no nuclear qualification. Infill qualification must start in 2027, with slag (S1) as the fallback (§8.6). At Liddell the barrier precedes any materials choice: none of these first-order barriers can be solved by changing Portland cement to LC³.

## 8.2 Economics, finance and market barriers

An SMR's cost is mostly capital, so whoever finances it sets its price and decides who can build one. C1 is the opposite: it costs almost nothing, but no one is paid for the carbon it saves.

**Cost.** Darlington's C$20.9 bn budget for four 300 MWe units (Ontario Power Generation, 2025a) gives a levelised cost of electricity (LCOE: the constant price that repays every cost over the plant's life) of C$198/MWh at a 7% real discount rate, and C$112–280 from 3% to 10% (Table T10). Capital is 65–86% of it, so finance terms matter as much as engineering. OPG's C$149/MWh implies 4.9%, a regulated Crown utility's cost of capital. Our A$221 at 7% sits just below GenCost's 2030 SMR range (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024), plausibly because four units share infrastructure. The agreement is not independent, because GenCost's current edition bases its SMR capital cost on Darlington (Graham and Hayward, 2026). A NuScale-size overrun would give C$317/MWh.

**Risk and finance.** NuScale's Idaho project shows what happens when buyers carry overrun risk: its price rose about 35% in real terms even after tax credits, the Utah utilities did not subscribe, and it was cancelled in 2023 (Table T10). Darlington avoids this with a single Crown owner and a regulated rate base; public and First Nations finance covers about 22% of the budget (§8.5). The first unit's C$7.7 bn, with common infrastructure, sits inside the C$20.9 bn, not on top of it. The Canada Growth Fund (up to C$2 bn) and Building Ontario Fund (up to C$1 bn) supply equity, and the C$715 m of loan guarantees behind the First Nations' investment is credit support, not extra spending (Government of Canada, 2026). The second equity tranche is released for Units 2–4 "once project milestones are met" (Canada Growth Fund and Building Ontario Fund, 2025), which is C1's main financial lever.

**Barriers to entry.** Entry needs large up-front capital, years of development, component qualification and regulatory approval; a small module is not proportionally cheap because site works, security and licensing are largely fixed (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2023). About C$17,400/kW is at risk for years before any revenue. Equity markets value US developers on hoped-for orders (NuScale Market capitalisation is a poor proxy for cost or performance: NuScale trades at 168 times its revenue; NuScale revenue (NuScale Power Corporation, 2026), but first 2026) because investors price hoped-for orders, not verified capital intensity or output. Darlington's disclosed budget is a stronger benchmark, though its operating record is yet to come. First plants are financed by states and rate bases: the US Department of Energy's US$800 m went to two designs, one the BWRX-300 (US Department of Energy, 2025), out of about 70 worldwide (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2024). China's State Council approves batches of reactors for state-owned builders, as reported (Caixin Global, 2026). Australia has neither route. Liddell belongs to AGL, a private company, so an SMR there would need Commonwealth ownership or underwriting, with taxpayers carrying the cost risk (our inference).

**C1.** LC³ saves 6,600 t CO₂e across Units 2–4 (2,200 t a unit; §6.3), worth C$0.63–0.76 m at the federal carbon price of C$95/t in 2026 and C$115/t in 2030 (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2026; BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023; Pillai et al., 2019). ## 8.3 Supply concentration and geopolitics

Every front-end fuel stage we measured is concentrated. Each Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI; §2) exceeds 1,800, "highly concentrated" on the 2023 US merger guidelines (US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission, 2023), though on the 2010 scale only enrichment is (Table T3). For Darlington this is a price risk; for an Australian SMR it is a sovereign risk.

**Sanctions make it worse.** Russia holds 41.0–42.8% of enrichment capacity (calculated from World Nuclear Association, 2025; World Nuclear University, 2025). Removing Russia, as US buyers must once import-ban waivers end in 2028 (United States Congress, 2024), raises every HHI. Without Russia and China, enrichment is a Urenco–Orano duopoly (HHI 5,621). Mining is concentrated by country but not by company (HHI about 850–1,400, top three firms only), so its risk is geopolitical rather than market power (Appendix B2).

**Darlington and Liddell compared.** OPG's announced Unit 1 fuel suppliers (Cameco, Urenco USA, Orano, GNF-A) include no Russian firm (Ontario Power Generation, 2023, as reported by Power Engineering, 2023). Four units need about 196 t of natural uranium and 138 thousand separative work units (SWU) a year, about 3.2% of Urenco USA's output (assumed typical BWR fuel values; Appendix B2.6). Australia holds 28% of identified recoverable uranium resources (OECD Nuclear Energy Agency and International Atomic Energy Agency, 2025; the 2026 edition gives 27–29%) but has no conversion or enrichment (World Nuclear Association, 2026). An Australian SMR would import enrichment and fabrication by law (EPBC s 140A; ARPANS s 10), and conversion because no plant exists (Parliamentary Library, 2024).

**Translation to practice.** C1 does not reduce fuel risk. Fuel independence also needs conversion, enrichment, fabrication, forged components and skilled labour, not uranium deposits alone. Patents identify proprietary designs but do not establish any royalty per unit of electricity, so patent counts alone give no royalty estimate. The HHI measures nominal capacity, not the contracts open to one buyer. Contracts with allied enrichers, as OPG uses, cut a buyer's exposure but not concentration (§8.6). C1 adds a small supply risk of its own. LC³ needs a suitable calcined-clay source and blending capacity (Scrivener et al., 2018), and a single qualified supplier would be a risk even if its product footprint were favourable. One Western option links back to Darlington: laser enrichment from Australia's Silex Systems, commercialised in the US by Global Laser Enrichment (51% Silex, 49% Cameco, OPG's uranium supplier). The NRC docketed its Paducah application in August 2025, as reported by Silex Systems Limited (2025), and Cameco has agreed to buy all its output (Silex Systems Limited, 2026, as reported by World Nuclear News, 2026). EPBC s 140A would bar such a plant in Australia (§10).

## 8.4 Social licence

The same reactor has a social licence at Darlington and none at Liddell. Safety data do not explain the difference; consent, benefit and trust do (Table T12).

**Perceived and actual safety.** In 2024, 61% of Australians supported using nuclear power to generate electricity (Neelam, 2024), yet 59% wanted to keep the legal ban, up from 51% a year earlier (Griffith University Climate Action Beacon and Monash Climate Change Communication Research Hub, 2025). One poll asks about a technology and the other about the law, so support depends on framing; older polls found support 16–20 points lower for a plant nearby (Newspoll, 2007). The health benefit, like the carbon benefit (§7), depends on what the reactor displaces.

**Consent: equity in Canada, failed top-down siting in Australia.** At Darlington the CNSC found the duty to consult met (CNSC, 2025). In June 2026 the seven Williams Treaties First Nations agreed to lend announced a C$700 m to the project, investment, a loan converting to equity once all four units are complete, guaranteed half each backed by federal and Ontario programmes loan guarantees of about C$715 m (Williams Treaties First Nations et al., 2026; Government of Canada, 2026). The first is the investment, the second its credit support. Two of the seven told the CNSC in 2023–24 that their concerns had not been addressed, so equity ownership by participating Nations is a means of not universal consent, not nor a substitute for it. consulting other Indigenous groups. Australia's precedent is a waste site chosen top-down and set aside by the Federal Court for apprehended bias (*Barngarla Determination Aboriginal Corporation RNTBC v Minister for Resources* [2023] FCA 809). Canada's waste agency instead used volunteer siting and a priced hosting agreement (Nuclear Waste Management Organization [NWMO], 2024a). Scored on legitimacy, legal risk, speed, public cost and fairness, consent-based routes beat top-down siting by 3–6 points under every weighting, and not building scores highest (Appendix B8.3).

**Translation to practice.** C1 changes no land use, emissions or waste, waste at the site, so it raises no new consent issue; issue there; its social value is indirect, as a lower-carbon build that OPG can report to its First Nations and public equity partners (our inference). Away from the site, clay sourcing, haulage and water use could create distributional impacts, and powder dust and calcination emissions are occupational matters at suppliers and batching plants. A socially sustainable pilot would disclose source locations and routes early, open supply to Indigenous and local firms where technically suitable, and report environmental data plainly. Waste stewardship is unchanged: the NWMO repository near Ignace entered regulatory assessment in January 2026 and is not operating (Nuclear Waste Management Organization, 2026), and LC³ has no proven recyclability or clearance advantage. For Liddell, low-regret steps can precede any repeal: a producer-funded waste body, and a two-key test in which both the host community and the Traditional Owners must agree. A priced hosting agreement, First Nations equity with a loan guarantee and a skills plan coordinated with AUKUS would follow (§8.6).

**Renewables rank first under every weight set.** The SMR wins only on life-cycle CO₂e (about 4 against 30 g/kWh) and supply concentration. It loses on CO₂e avoided per dollar (10.9×), cost (2.9×), time (about 12 years later), social licence and legal feasibility.

**Rank reversal.** Of 56 weight changes of ±50%, two change first place, both under the firm-and-sovereign weights and both to the status quo, not the SMR. An SMR ranks first only if life-cycle CO₂e carries at least 94% of the weight, or in 0.1% of 20,000 random weight sets. Of 68 input tests, renewables stay first in 66; the other two go to the status quo.

**SMR + C1 and SMR are indistinguishable.** At 2,200 t per unit (50,000 m³ of concrete, 40% eligible; BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023; Pillai et al., 2019), C1 moves the weighted score by under 0.005 point, and the ranking is unchanged. At Liddell, an 18.5-day slip would forgo CO₂e equal to 15 times the saving (Appendix B7.5), so schedule neutrality decides whether C1 helps.

**The status quo's second place over the SMR is a scaling artefact.** It scores full marks on cost, supply and legal feasibility because it changes nothing; on fixed global scales the SMR moves above it. Renewables stay first on both.

### 9.3 Verdict and limits

**Darlington.** C1 is justified as a controlled innovation within a credible nuclear construction programme, not as an argument that all SMRs should be built. Pilot LC³ in eligible non-safety works first; use steel-composite infill only if qualified on Unit 1 mock-ups, otherwise specify the slag blend. Independent assurance is a safeguard against trading a carbon saving for structural or occupational risk. Qualification must start in 2027 for the 2028 Units 2–4 decision (§8.6).

**Australia.** An SMR at Liddell would need its cost gap closed, first power well before 2040, and the legal and consent barriers removed first (Appendix B3, route C). We would keep SMRs under review against Darlington's outturn cost.

**Limits.** The social, legal and renewables-supply scores and all four weight sets are our judgement. The status quo's lead over the SMR depends on the scaling; only "renewables first" is robust to it. Schedule risk enters through first power (2040 at the earliest; the Coalition's 2035 and a 2045 case change nothing). Costs are GenCost's (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024); the renewables supply score uses solar PV alone (International Energy Agency, 2022).
