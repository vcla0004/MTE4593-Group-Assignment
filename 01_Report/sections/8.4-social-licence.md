## 8.4 Social licence

The same reactor has a social licence at Darlington and none at Liddell. Safety data do not explain the difference; consent, benefit and trust do (Table T12).

**Perceived and actual safety.** In 2024, 61% of Australians supported using nuclear power to generate electricity (Neelam, 2024), yet 59% wanted to keep the legal ban, up from 51% a year earlier (Griffith University Climate Action Beacon and Monash Climate Change Communication Research Hub, 2025). One poll asks about a technology and the other about the law, so support depends on framing. The health benefit, like the carbon benefit (§7), depends on what the reactor displaces.

**Consent: equity in Canada, failed top-down siting in Australia.** At Darlington the CNSC found the duty to consult met (CNSC, 2025). In June 2026 the seven Williams Treaties First Nations announced a C$700 m investment, a loan converting to equity once all four units are complete, backed by federal and Ontario loan guarantees of about C$715 m (Williams Treaties First Nations et al., 2026; Government of Canada, 2026). Two of the seven told the CNSC in 2023–24 that their concerns had not been addressed (Mississaugas of Scugog Island First Nation, 2023; Curve Lake First Nation, 2024), so ownership is not universal consent. Australia's precedent is a waste site chosen top-down and set aside by the Federal Court for apprehended bias (*Barngarla Determination Aboriginal Corporation RNTBC v Minister for Resources* [2023] FCA 809); Canada's waste agency used volunteer siting and a priced hosting agreement (Nuclear Waste Management Organization [NWMO], 2024a). Scored on legitimacy, legal risk, speed, public cost and fairness, consent-based routes beat top-down siting by 3–6 points under every weighting, and not building scores highest (Appendix B8.3).

**Translation to practice.** C1 changes no land use, emissions or waste at the site, so it raises no new consent issue there; its social value is indirect, as a lower-carbon build that OPG can report to its First Nations and public equity partners (our inference). A socially sustainable pilot would disclose clay source locations and routes early, open supply to Indigenous and local firms where suitable, and report environmental data plainly. Waste stewardship is unchanged: the NWMO repository near Ignace entered regulatory assessment in January 2026 and is not operating (Nuclear Waste Management Organization, 2026). For Liddell, low-regret steps can precede any repeal: a producer-funded waste body and a two-key test in which both the host community and the Traditional Owners must agree, then a priced hosting agreement, First Nations equity with a loan guarantee and a skills plan coordinated with AUKUS (§8.6).
