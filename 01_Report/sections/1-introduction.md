## 1 Introduction

**When a coal plant closes, someone must replace its firm output, and small modular reactors (SMRs) are one proposed answer. We ask whether an SMR is feasible across the triple bottom line (environmental, economic and social), and whether a materials choice can make it cleaner without making it later.**

**Context.** Liddell in New South Wales (NSW), 2,000 MW, was approved to close by April 2023 (Australian Energy Regulator, 2021) and has since closed; the NSW grid still emits about 640 g CO₂e/kWh (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2025). Ontario no longer burns coal for power and emits about 74 g/kWh (The Atmospheric Fund, 2025). An SMR is a reactor of up to about 300 MWe built largely from factory-made modules (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2024). The GE Vernova Hitachi BWRX-300 is the first SMR licensed for construction in an OECD country (Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, 2025).

**The two sites.** *Darlington, Ontario* is the initiative site: Ontario Power Generation (OPG) plans four units for C$20.9 billion, with Unit 1 in service in 2030 (Ontario Power Generation, 2025). Units 2–4 need further licensing (§8.1). *Liddell* is the Australian comparison, a closed coal site with a grid connection. The technology and projects are in §4.1–4.2.

**The initiative (C1).** OPG should pilot limestone calcined clay cement (LC³) in eligible non-safety works first, and use it in the steel-composite infill only if qualified, and only if it adds no delay (§4.3; materials in §5).

**Research questions.**
1. What does one BWRX-300 emit over its life, and where? (§6)
2. How much does C1 save, and what delay cancels it? (§6–7)
3. Is a unit's climate value different in Ontario and at Liddell? (§7)
4. Which barriers decide whether an SMR is built, and does it beat firmed renewables and the status quo? (§3, §8–9)
