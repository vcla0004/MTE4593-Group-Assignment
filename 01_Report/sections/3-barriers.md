## 3 Barriers to cutting emissions with small modular reactors

**Seven barriers decide whether a small modular reactor (SMR) is built and whether it cuts emissions. The initiative, C1 (§4.3), reaches only one of them directly, and a second sets its terms.** Table T15 gives each barrier with one number and one source, then states whether C1 touches it. The barriers are not equal. At Darlington, Ontario, the binding ones are cost and schedule, because law, regulator and owner are already aligned. At Liddell, the closed New South Wales (NSW) coal plant we use as the Australian comparison, law comes first and every later barrier is still open (§8.1–8.5).

*Table T15. Barriers to SMR deployment, one number each, and whether initiative C1 touches them.*
*Source: as cited in each row; calculations in `03_LCA_Calculations/results/` (Appendix B); sections cited for detail. All figures [TO VERIFY] until each primary is opened.*

| Barrier | Number and source | Why it matters | Does C1 touch it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cost and finance | **C$198/MWh** at a 7% real discount rate for Darlington's C$20.9 bn, four-unit budget (our calculation from Ontario Power Generation, 2025; §8.2) | Capital is 65–86% of the cost, so whoever finances it sets the price. NuScale's Idaho project was cancelled in 2023 when buyers carried the overrun risk | **No.** C1's target concrete is about 0.07% of Units 2–4 capital (§8.2) |
| 2. Time to deploy | **Unit 1 in service October 2030**, against **2040 at the earliest** for Australia (Ontario Power Generation, 2025b; Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024; §8.1) | Late plants displace less. Each year a Liddell unit is delayed forgoes some of the CO₂e it would avoid (§7) | **Yes, as a constraint.** C1 must not add delay (§4.3) |
| 3. Law | **36 Senate votes at most** against 39 needed to repeal the federal bans (EPBC Act s 140A and ARPANS Act s 10) (Parliament of Australia, 2026; §8.1) | Federal repeal would still leave NSW's own ban, a criminal offence for a private owner | **No.** Darlington is licensed; the bans matter only for Liddell |
| 4. Fuel-supply concentration | **Enrichment HHI 3,009**, against 1,800 for "highly concentrated" (calculated from World Nuclear Association, 2025; US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission, 2023; §8.3) | Russia holds 42.8% of enrichment capacity. The Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) is the sum of squared market shares. Darlington faces a price risk, Australia a sovereign one | **No.** C1 does not change the fuel chain |
| 5. Embodied carbon and nuclear qualification | **7.5%**: construction's share of life-cycle emissions; the fuel front end is 64% (this study, §6.2) | Embodied carbon is a small share, and infill concrete must be qualified for nuclear use before Units 2–4 are fixed. No nuclear precedent was found for calcined-clay infill (GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024; §8.1) | **Yes.** This is the barrier C1 targets |
| 6. Social licence | **59%** of Australians wanted the ban kept in 2025, though 61% supported nuclear electricity in 2024 (Griffith University Climate Action Beacon and Monash Climate Change Communication Research Hub, 2025; §8.4) | Support is soft and depends on framing. Canada has a funded route to First Nations consent; Australia has none | **No.** C1 adds no new consent issue |
| 7. Workforce | **76,950** shortfall across four trades by 2030–31 (Defence Connect, n.d.; §8.4) | A civil plant would compete with the submarine programme for the same trades | **No.** Darlington's workforce is established |

**Reading the table.** The barriers that dominate (1 to 4) are ones C1 cannot move. What C1 can do is small: it saves about 0.3% of a unit's life-cycle emissions (§6.3). We therefore treat C1 as a low-cost addition to a plant that is built for other reasons, and §7 and §8.6 test whether it is worth the schedule risk. The climate benefit of the SMR itself depends on what it displaces, not on any of these barriers (§7).
