## 8.2 Economics, finance and market barriers

An SMR's cost is mostly capital, so whoever finances it sets its price and decides who can build one. C1 is the opposite: it costs almost nothing, but no one is paid for the carbon it saves.

**Cost.** Darlington's C$20.9 bn budget for four 300 MWe units (Ontario Power Generation, 2025) gives a levelised cost of electricity (LCOE: the constant price that repays every cost over the plant's life) of C$198/MWh at a 7% real discount rate, and C$112–280 from 3% to 10% (Table T10). Capital is 65–86% of it, so finance terms matter as much as engineering. OPG's C$149/MWh implies 4.9%, a regulated Crown utility's cost of capital. Our A$221 at 7% sits just below GenCost's 2030 SMR range (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024), plausibly because four units share infrastructure. The agreement is not independent, because GenCost's current edition bases its SMR capital cost on Darlington (Graham and Hayward, 2026). A NuScale-size overrun would give C$317/MWh.

**Risk and finance.** NuScale's Idaho project shows what happens when buyers carry overrun risk: its price rose about 35% in real terms even after tax credits, the Utah utilities did not subscribe, and it was cancelled in 2023 (Table T10). Darlington avoids this with a single Crown owner and a regulated rate base; public and First Nations finance covers about 22% of the budget (§8.5). The second equity tranche is released for Units 2–4 "once project milestones are met" (Canada Growth Fund and Building Ontario Fund, 2025), which is C1's main financial lever.

**Barriers to entry.** About C$17,400/kW is at risk for years before any revenue. Equity markets value US developers on hoped-for orders (NuScale at 168 times its revenue; NuScale Power Corporation, 2026), but first plants are financed by states and rate bases: the US Department of Energy's US$800 m went to two designs, one the BWRX-300 (US Department of Energy, 2025), out of about 70 worldwide (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2024). China's State Council approves batches of reactors for state-owned builders, as reported (Caixin Global, 2026). Australia has neither route. Liddell belongs to AGL, a private company, so an SMR there would need Commonwealth ownership or underwriting, with taxpayers carrying the cost risk (our inference).

**C1.** LC³ saves 6,600 t CO₂e across Units 2–4 (2,200 t a unit; §6.3), worth C$0.63–0.76 m at the federal carbon price of C$95/t in 2026 and C$115/t in 2030 (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2026; BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023; Pillai et al., 2019). That justifies an LC³ premium of up to C$10.4–12.7/m³, or 4.6–5.5% of commodity concrete at C$229/m³. The target concrete costs about C$13.7 m, 0.10% of Units 2–4 capital. No one captures the carbon value, because construction materials appear to sit outside Ontario's industrial carbon pricing (our inference). Qualification sets the abatement cost: a C$1 m programme (assumed) adds about C$152/t, above the C$95–115/t price path, and one day of delay costs C$1.8–2.9 m, more than C1's whole carbon value. Responses are compared and sequenced in §8.6 (Table T16).
