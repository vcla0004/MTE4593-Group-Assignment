## 8.1 Law, regulation and licensing

Law is where Darlington and Liddell differ most. At Darlington, C1 needs a qualified mix, accepted as a design change before Units 2–4 are approved to proceed, a milestone expected in 2028 (American Nuclear Society, 2026) [TO VERIFY]. At Liddell, three laws and the site owner stand in the way, and on current party positions none of the laws will be repealed in this Parliament (Table T7).

**Australia.** Federal repeal needs 39 Senate votes if all 76 senators vote. If One Nation and Senator Babet join the Coalition (our assumption), the pro-repeal bloc has 32 votes, while Labor and the Greens (40) oppose repeal (Parliament of Australia, 2026; Bowen, 2025; Australian Greens, 2025) [TO VERIFY] [TODO: Appendix B — Senate arithmetic and route scores]. The Coalition's pledge to "repeal all legislative prohibitions" therefore cannot pass unless Labor or the Greens change position (ABC News, 2026; Liberal Party of Australia, 2026) [TO VERIFY]. Repeal alone would not be enough. ARPANSA, the federal radiation regulator, licenses only Commonwealth-controlled bodies (ARPANSA, n.d.) [TO VERIFY], so a private plant would fall under NSW law. The NSW Act exempts only the Commonwealth's successors to the former Australian Atomic Energy Commission, such as ANSTO's research reactor at Lucas Heights (s 8(3)) [TO VERIFY].

**Canada.** Even at a licensed site with a willing government, the licence to construct one BWRX-300 took about 29 months. It was issued 17.9 years (elapsed time, not continuous review) after the environmental assessment began (Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission [CNSC], 2025; 2026a) [TO VERIFY]. For C1 the obstacle is qualification, not the cement standard, which already allows calcined-clay blends with up to 15% limestone (CSA Group, 2023; Hooton, 2023) [TO VERIFY]. We found no nuclear precedent for calcined clay as self-consolidating infill in steel-plate composite modules. The CNSC and the US NRC have jointly reviewed the BWRX-300's module design approach and found it acceptable (GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024; US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and CNSC, 2025) [TO VERIFY].

**Translation to practice.** C1 stays schedule-neutral only if the mix is qualified on a full-scale mock-up during Unit 1 construction, with slag (S1) as the fallback (§8.6). An Australian SMR has no legal start date, so §7 treats CSIRO's 2040 "earliest" date as optimistic (Graham, Hayward and Foster, 2024) [TO VERIFY: covers SMRs?]. It would need every action in Table T7 and an independent regulator early in the programme (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2015) [TO VERIFY edition].

### Note for §9 (MCDA input; moved out of the §8.1 text to meet the page budget)

There are three routes to a licensable SMR in Australia (law brief §5) [TO VERIFY]:
- **A.** A Commonwealth-owned plant under ARPANSA (the Coalition's 2024 model). The current citation, ABC News (2024), is a weak secondary source; replace it with the Coalition's 19 June 2024 release (R1.4). This route is the quickest but the easiest to reverse.
- **B.** A federal licensing law for private operators that overrides the states under Constitution s 109. Partlett warns of a High Court challenge (Canberra Times, 2025).
- **C.** State repeal plus a state or joint regulator. This is the most durable route but the hardest to achieve.

These scores are our own qualitative judgement (1 = poor to 3 = good, across four objectives), not sourced scores. The base totals are A 7, B 5 and C 8 out of 12. Weighting regulator readiness double gives A 10, B 7, C 9; weighting durability double gives A 8, B 6, C 11. The ranking therefore depends on whether speed or durability is valued more. Under the 2026 Senate, no route is open. [TODO: Appendix B — Senate arithmetic and route scores]
