## 4.3 The initiative: low-clinker concrete for the Darlington Units 2–4 infill

**C1 targets a materials problem that modular construction creates, and it is worth doing only if it is schedule-neutral.** The BWRX-300's reactor building is made of diaphragm-plate steel-composite (DPSC) modules filled on site with concrete. The infill must flow between closely spaced plates, so we assume a cement-rich self-consolidating mix (our inference; GE-Hitachi Nuclear Energy, 2024). A delay of 11.6–18.5 days to one unit on Ontario's average grid, or 1.7–1.8 days on its marginal grid, cancels that unit's saving (§7.1).

**The proposal.** Ontario Power Generation (OPG) should pilot limestone calcined clay cement (LC³; Scrivener et al., 2018) in eligible non-safety works first, and use it in the infill only if qualified on Unit 1 mock-ups; Units 2–4 need future licensing (§8.1, §8.6). The Ostrołęka BWRX-300 design uses 50,000 m³ of concrete per unit (BWRX-300 Ostrołęka sp. z o.o., 2023). If 20%, 40% or 60% is eligible, replacing ordinary cement with LC³ (380 to 270 kg CO₂e/m³; Pillai et al., 2019) saves 1,100, 2,200 or 3,300 t CO₂e per unit (40% central), and 6,600 t across Units 2–4. A qualified slag blend is the fallback.

**Why it is not the obvious choice.** C1's value is set by schedule, not by the concrete: under three weeks of delay to a unit cancels its saving (§7.1), so C1 is a qualification programme timed to Unit 1 mock-ups. Its lever is financial: the Units 2–4 equity tranche can carry the condition (§8.6).

**What C1 does not address.** Cost, law, fuel supply, social licence and workforce (Table T15); lower-carbon steel is the next lever (§6.3, §10).
